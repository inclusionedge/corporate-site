Ask most HR leaders whether their inclusion training changed behaviour, and you will get a pause before the answer. That pause is the entire problem.

Frank Dobbin and Alexandra Kalev spent close to three decades tracking diversity practices across several hundred organisations. Their central finding is uncomfortable for an industry that sells workshops: mandatory, compliance-driven, awareness-only training is among the least effective interventions available — and in some cases it provokes the very defensiveness it was meant to prevent. People do not respond well to being told what to think. They respond, sometimes, to being shown what to do.

## Awareness was never the deliverable

A workshop can teach someone the definition of neurodivergence in forty-five minutes. It cannot teach a manager what to do when a team member asks for instructions in writing rather than verbally, in a meeting that is already running late, in front of three other people waiting to speak.

That gap — between knowing a concept and holding a behaviour under real conditions — is where most training programmes quietly stop, and where the actual work begins.

> Training that ends at awareness has manufactured a feeling of progress without the artefact that proves it: behaviour that holds under pressure, weeks later, when nobody is watching.

## Three design choices, made deliberately against the failure pattern

**Behavioural focus over conceptual coverage.** CARE is built around four observable behaviours — Consider, Acknowledge, Respect, Empathy — rather than a syllabus of definitions. Participants leave with things to do, not only things to know.

**Business framing over moral appeal.** The case is made in terms of collaboration friction, avoidable misunderstanding and team performance, rather than framed as a moral test employees are being asked to pass. The research suggests this distinction matters more than most programme designers assume.

**Embedded tiers over a single session.** CARE does not stand alone. It is the foundation of a ladder continuing into ENGAGE for managers and ENABLE for senior leaders. A culture shift that only reaches the all-staff layer has no mechanism to survive a manager who was never equipped for the harder conversation, or a leader who never resourced it.

## Voluntary framing, measured rigorously

One further pattern from the research deserves more attention than it gets: voluntary or opt-in framing tends to outperform mandatory compliance framing, even when the content is identical.

CARE is designed as something people choose to engage with fully — assessed through a knowledge check and a personal commitment statement rather than simply attendance — instead of something endured.
