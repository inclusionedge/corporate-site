Ask a room full of managers to "be more empathetic" and you have asked them to do something you cannot verify, cannot coach, and cannot assess. That is not a training design. It is a hope.

Empathy is the E in CARE — Consider, Acknowledge, Respect, Empathy — and it is the element most training programmes get wrong, because most treat it as an emotional state: something a manager either has or does not, felt rather than done. That framing is a dead end for organisational learning, because emotional states are not a competency you can build, observe or certify.

## The cognitive alternative

CARE frames empathy cognitively instead — as the deliberate act of seeking to understand a colleague's perspective and experience before forming judgement.

That is a sequence of observable steps, not a feeling: pausing before reacting, asking a clarifying question instead of assuming intent, and holding a provisional interpretation rather than a settled one until more information arrives.

> Cognitive empathy is trainable precisely because it is not a feeling. It is a sequence — pause, ask, hold judgement — and every step in that sequence can be practised, coached and observed.

## Why this distinction matters for measurement

Impact measurement leans on Kirkpatrick's Levels 3 and 4 — behaviour change and organisational results — rather than stopping at how a workshop made someone feel. That is only possible if the underlying construct is behavioural to begin with.

A follow-up pulse can ask whether a manager paused to ask a clarifying question in a recent disagreement. It cannot meaningfully ask whether they felt more empathetic, because there is no shared, observable referent behind the question.

## What this looks like in practice

In the CARE workshop, empathy is taught alongside respect, as the pairing that resolves the tension between flexibility and standards — the moment where someone has to reconcile *I want to support this person* with *I still have a deadline, a team, and expectations to hold.*

Cognitive empathy gives them a concrete move in that moment: understand the constraint the other person is working inside before deciding how to respond to it.

That is a skill. It can be practised badly, practised well, and — critically — it can be measured.
