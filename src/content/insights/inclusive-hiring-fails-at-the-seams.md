Ask an organisation why its inclusive hiring initiative underdelivered, and the diagnosis usually lands on a person or a stage. The interview panel needed training. The job advert used the wrong language. The manager was not bought in.

In practice, the failure is almost never located inside a stage. It happens between them.

## The custodian problem

A hiring process is a relay. A recruiter holds the candidate through sourcing and screening. A hiring manager takes over for interview and selection. Someone in HR operations handles offer and contracting. An onboarding coordinator picks it up from there. Eventually the team itself becomes the custodian, permanently.

Each of those people may be entirely committed to inclusive practice. And the process can still fail — because commitment is held by individuals, while context is lost in transfer.

The recruiter knows the candidate asked for interview questions in advance and that it made a visible difference to their performance. Does the hiring manager know? The hiring manager knows the candidate will work best with written task briefs. Does the onboarding coordinator know? The onboarding coordinator knows the new joiner needs a quiet space for the first fortnight. Does the team know, and did anyone ask the candidate whether they wanted that shared?

> Inclusive hiring rarely fails because someone opposed it. It fails because the person who knew was not the person who acted, and nothing carried the knowledge across the gap.

## Why seams are invisible in the metrics

Most hiring dashboards measure stages. Time-to-hire, offer acceptance rate, ninety-day retention. Stage metrics are structurally incapable of showing you a handoff failure, because the failure does not occur inside any stage — it occurs in the whitespace between two of them, which nobody instrumented.

The result is an organisation that can see its inclusive hiring numbers declining without being able to locate the cause, and so defaults to the only explanation its data supports: someone in one of the stages must not be trying hard enough.

## What actually closes a seam

Three things, none of them expensive:

**A named custodian for the transfer itself.** Not the sender, not the receiver — someone accountable for the fact of transfer having happened. In small organisations this is often the same person for every seam, which is fine, provided it is explicit.

**A consent-governed record of what matters.** Information about a person's support needs cannot simply be forwarded because it would be operationally convenient. It travels with consent, at the level of detail the person agreed to, and no further. Getting this wrong is a governance failure, not a helpful shortcut.

**A structured question at the receiving end.** The receiving custodian should not be left to infer. A single deliberate prompt — *what do I need to know to set this person up well, and what has already been agreed* — closes more seams than any amount of unconscious bias training.

## The uncomfortable part

Seams are unglamorous. No one gets promoted for improving a handoff. There is no launch event for a consent-governed transfer protocol. It is exactly the kind of work that gets deprioritised in favour of something more visible, which is precisely why it remains broken in most organisations.

That is also why it is where the return is.
