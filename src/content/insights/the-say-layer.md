A quiet design mistake shows up across a great deal of inclusion training: the scripts are written for the wrong person.

It is common to see workshop material that hands the neurodivergent employee a script — how to disclose a need, how to explain a difference, how to request an adjustment without sounding difficult. The intention is usually good. The effect is that the burden of translation stays exactly where it has always sat: with the person who holds the least structural power in the interaction, and the most to lose if the conversation goes badly.

## Think, Say, Do — and who each layer belongs to

The Think / Say / Do method runs through every tier of the CARE, ENGAGE and ENABLE ladder.

**Think** is the internal cognitive shift — noticing an assumption before it hardens into a reaction.

**Do** is the observable action, built wherever possible around universal design, so that an adjustment does not require singling anyone out.

**Say** is the layer most often assigned to the wrong party. In this framework, Say is social scripting for the supporter — the colleague, the manager, the leader — not for the person being supported. It answers a different question than most training asks: not *how should a neurodivergent employee explain themselves*, but *what does a manager actually say, out loud, in the moment a need surfaces.*

> The person disclosing a need has usually spent years working out how to ask. The person receiving that disclosure has usually had no equivalent practice at all — and it shows.

## Why this matters for managers specifically

This is precisely the gap ENGAGE exists to close. A manager who has never rehearsed a response defaults to silence, over-formality, or an anxious over-correction — none of which count as inclusive conduct, however well-intentioned.

Giving the supporter a workable script, tested against realistic scenarios rather than invented ones, is what turns a good policy into something that actually gets said out loud on a Tuesday afternoon.

## Universal design as the default "Do"

The corollary is that Do should rarely require a visible adjustment aimed at one person.

Written follow-ups after verbal briefings, predictable meeting structures, and unambiguous task instructions tend to help considerably more of the workforce than the one colleague who happened to ask for them first — which is also why they are easier for a manager to introduce without drawing attention to any individual.
